\quad $0\oplus0=1\oplus1=0$,\quad $0\oplus1=1\oplus0=1$.

% 3 | 04:00--05:30
\begin{frame}[label=slide3]{Affine Boolean functions define the codewords}
\talktext
A \emph{Boolean function} assigns one bit to each binary address.
Our encoder uses an \emph{affine} function: a constant XOR selected input bits.
\[
f_{a,b}(u)=b\oplus a_1u_1\oplus a_2u_2\oplus a_3u_3.
\]
Let $e_i$ be the address with a $1$ in position $i$ and zeros elsewhere:
$e_1=100$, $e_2=010$, $e_3=001$.
An uncorrupted word gives back its message:
\[
b=f_{a,b}(000),\qquad a_i=f_{a,b}(e_i)\oplus f_{a,b}(000).
\]
Different messages therefore give different stored words.
These $16$ allowed words are the \emph{codewords}; their collection is the \emph{code} $C$.
\takeaway{$C$ selects 16 words from the 256 possible eight-bit readouts.}
\note{04:00--05:30. Prerequisite: The encoder formula and address order. Terms: Boolean function; affine; e1=100,e2=010,e3=001; codeword; C. Narration: A Boolean function is the complete input-output table. An affine function is a constant XOR selected input bits. Introduce ei before the recovery formula. The value at zero gives b; comparing zero with ei gives ai. Therefore no two messages encode to the same word. The receiver knows this family because it knows the encoder. Reveals: One state. Read definitions, then demonstrate the recovery formulas, then infer sixteen distinct codewords. Transition: Now inspect the actual readout as a function too.}
\end{frame}

% 4 | 05:30--07:30
\begin{frame}[label=slide4]{From a truth table to a polynomial}
\talktext
Our example is parity, $p(u)=u_1\oplus u_2\oplus u_3$.
The function $r$ records the eight bits read from memory:
\[
p\longleftrightarrow\mathtt{01101001},\qquad
r\longleftrightarrow\textcolor{Gold}{\mathtt{1}}\mathtt{1101001}.
\]
\begin{uncoverenv}<2->
The product $(1\oplus u_1)(1\oplus u_2)(1\oplus u_3)$ equals $1$ at $000$ and $0$ at every other address. Hence
\[
r(u)=p(u)\oplus(1\oplus u_1)(1\oplus u_2)(1\oplus u_3).
\]
\end{uncoverenv}
\begin{uncoverenv}<3->
The unique XOR of products of distinct input variables is the
\emph{algebraic normal form} (ANF). Expanding gives
\[
r(u)=1\oplus u_1u_2\oplus u_1u_3\oplus u_2u_3\oplus u_1u_2u_3.
\]
The \emph{degree} is the largest number of variables in a product term.
Codewords have degree at most one; this readout has degree three.
\end{uncoverenv}
\note{05:30--07:30. Prerequisite: The affine encoder and the single changed entry. Terms: p: parity; r: readout; algebraic normal form (ANF); degree. Narration: Point first to p and r. At 000 every factor of (1 XOR u1)(1 XOR u2)(1 XOR u3) is one; elsewhere some factor vanishes. Expanding cancels parity's linear terms. ANF is a description of the damaged table, not a step the decoder must perform. Degree counts variables in the largest product term, not errors. Reveals: State 1: original and corrupted words. State 2: construct the indicator product. State 3: name ANF, expand, and compare degree with the code family. Transition: To decode, compare neighboring table entries.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[label=slide7]{Intersections count differing positions}
\talktext
The \emph{support} is the set of positions containing a one.
The \emph{weight} $\wt(f)$ counts those positions.
\begin{columns}[c,onlytextwidth]
\column{.42\textwidth}\centering
\resizebox{!}{27mm}{\facepicture}
\par\scriptsize Labels are addresses. Colored faces mark ones; the dark edge is their intersection.
\column{.55\textwidth}
\[
\wt(u_1)=4,\quad\wt(u_2)=4,
\qquad\wt(u_1u_2)=2.
\]
XOR keeps positions in exactly one support.
The common edge was counted twice and must be removed twice.
\end{columns}
\begin{uncoverenv}<2->
\[
\wt(f\oplus g)=\wt(f)+\wt(g)-2\wt(fg).
\]
\emph{Hamming distance} counts positions where two words differ:
\[
d_H(f,g)=\wt(f\oplus g),\qquad d_H(u_1,u_2)=4+4-2(2)=4.
\]
\end{uncoverenv}
\note{11:30--13:15. Prerequisite: Products mean AND; XOR indicates disagreement. Terms: Support in words; wt(f); Hamming distance d\_H(f,g). Narration: The support is the positions with value one; weight counts them. Point to the two colored coordinate faces and common edge. Four plus four counts the intersection twice, whereas XOR excludes it. Subtract two copies of the two-point overlap. Weight arithmetic is ordinary integer arithmetic. Reveals: State 1: faces, counts, and overlap reasoning. State 2: general identity, definition of pairwise distance, and d\_H(u1,u2)=4. Transition: The same four-versus-four count holds for any nonconstant affine function.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[label=slide9]{Minimum distance makes correction unique}
\talktext
The \emph{minimum distance} separates the closest distinct codewords:
\[
d_{\min}(C)=\min_{\substack{f,g\in C\\f\ne g}}d_H(f,g).
\]
Their nonzero affine difference $f\oplus g$ has four ones if nonconstant,
or eight ones if constant. The pair $u_1,u_2$ attains four, so $d_{\min}(C)=4$.
\begin{uncoverenv}<2->
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\small]
\node (f) at (0,0) {codeword $f$};\node (r) at (4,0) {readout $r$};\node (g) at (8,0) {codeword $g$};
\draw[<->,thick,Teal] (f)--node[above]{at most 1 change}(r);
\draw[<->,thick,Teal] (r)--node[above]{at most 1 change}(g);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Two different candidates would imply
\[
4\le d_H(f,g)\le d_H(f,r)+d_H(r,g)\le2.
\]
One-error correction is unique.
\end{uncoverenv}
\note{15:00--16:45. Prerequisite: Every nonconstant affine function has four ones. Terms: d\_min(C), distinct from d\_H(f,g). Narration: The XOR of two distinct affine words is nonzero affine: four ones if nonconstant, eight if constant. The earlier coordinate pair attains four, so the minimum is exactly four. If two candidates were each within one change of r, joining those changes would put them within two of one another. This contradicts their minimum separation. Reveals: State 1: define and establish d\_min(C)=4. State 2: two hypothetical candidates, the distance chain, and unique correction. Transition: Review what is now fully established before changing physical setting.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[label=slide11]{A quantum state has amplitudes and relative phase}
\talktext
A qubit uses two basis vectors, written
$\ket0=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\ket1=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$.
Their coefficients are \emph{amplitudes}; the squared magnitudes sum to one.
\[
\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}
\quad\text{is a superposition of the two basis states.}
\]
\begin{uncoverenv}<2->
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Change one relative sign}
\[
\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\longmapsto
\frac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}.
\]
This phase flip changes the state.

\begin{frame}[label=backupAffine]{Affine equivalence changes input coordinates}
\hypertarget{backup-affine}{}\talktext
\textbf{Question from slide 8:} Does changing binary coordinates change these counts or the code?

For $m$-bit inputs, let $A$ be an invertible binary matrix and $t$ an $m$-bit translation. Matrix addition uses XOR.
Input-affine equivalence means
\[
g(u)=f(Au\oplus t).
\]
This bijection permutes table entries, preserving weight and polynomial degree (for nonzero functions). Applied to both words, it preserves Hamming distance.

For a direction $v$, the derivatives transform as
\[
D_vg(u)=D_{Av}f(Au\oplus t).
\]
Use the same address permutation for every codeword. The relabeled classical code has the same distances and correction guarantee.

\begin{frame}[label=backupToy]{The eight-qubit quantum distance is two}
\hypertarget{backup-toy}{}\talktext
\textbf{Question from slide 16:} Why does the toy code not correct every one-qubit error?

A \emph{stabilizer check} tests an operation that fixes every encoded state.
The toy code's checks detect every single-site bit or phase error.
Write $Z_u$ for a phase flip at physical address $u$.
\[
Z_{000}Z_{100}\ket a_L=(-1)^{a_1}\ket a_L.
\]
This two-site error passes every check. Both words within a pair receive the same phase, but the phase depends on the logical label.

Quantum distance counts the fewest sites in an undetected nontrivial logical bit/phase error. Here it is two: detection of one-site errors does not imply correction of an unknown one-site error.

\begin{frame}[label=backupSigns]{The 48-qubit rotation-sign polynomial}
\hypertarget{backup-signs}{}\talktext
\textbf{Question from slide 20:} How is the rotation pattern specified?

For the four within-block bits $u$ and two block bits $w$, define
\begin{align*}
Q_1(u)&=u_1\oplus u_2\oplus u_3\oplus u_1u_3\oplus u_2u_3\oplus u_2u_4,\\
Q_2(u)&=u_3\oplus u_4\oplus u_1u_2\oplus u_1u_4\oplus u_3u_4.
\end{align*}
The bit-valued sign function is
\[
\gamma(u,w)=u_3\oplus w_1Q_1(u)\oplus w_2Q_2(u).
\]
Value zero selects $T$; value one selects $T^\dagger$.
On blocks $10,01,11$, the numbers of ones are $6,6,10$, giving $22$ inverse and $26$ forward rotations.

\begin{frame}[label=backupBound]{The scope of the length exclusion}
\hypertarget{backup-bound}{}\talktext
\textbf{Question from slide 23:} Is 48 the smallest possible length?

Hypotheses: \emph{CSS codes} (separate bit-flip and phase-flip checks), three logical qubits, and $d_Z\ge3$ (no undetected phase-type logical error on one or two sites).

Also require the \emph{Campbell--Howard conditions}: nine even/odd requirements on rows and their pair/triple intersections.

$n=48$ & the displayed construction implements that pattern
\end{tabular}
